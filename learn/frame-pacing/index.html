Why 100 FPS can still stutter

A beginner-friendly guide to FPS, frame times, 1% lows and why smoothness depends on more than an average.

LINUX OBSERVATORY · EXPLANATORY GUIDE · UPDATED NOVEMBER 2026 · NO BENCHMARK DATA USED

A game says 100 FPS. The counter looks impressive. Yet when you turn the camera, motion suddenly jolts. How can both things be true?

FPS is an average, not a promise.

A game does not show continuous motion. It creates individual pictures—frames—and the display shows them in sequence. FPS means frames per second. A reading of 100 FPS suggests that roughly a hundred frames were produced over the measurement window. It does not say that the space between those frames was perfectly even.

Imagine two drummers playing sixty beats in one minute. One plays steadily; the other suddenly pauses and then catches up. Their beat count is the same, but they do not sound the same. That is the useful intuition behind frame pacing.

Frame time gives you a closer look.

A frame-time reading describes the duration associated with an individual frame, usually in milliseconds. At a perfectly even 60 FPS, the interval works out to about 16.7 ms per frame (1,000 ÷ 60). At an even 100 FPS it would be 10 ms.

But real workloads change from frame to frame. A new area loads; a shader is compiled; the CPU performs a difficult task; a graphics driver stalls; or the display and presentation chain introduce an irregular interval. The overlay may show a peak long after the average FPS reading has recovered.

When investigating a hitch, look for timing spikes and consider what was happening in the game at that moment. The observation is a starting point; the graph alone does not identify the cause.

What does “1% low” mean?

A 1% low tries to summarize the slower end of performance rather than the typical rate. However, tools and publications do not always use the same computation. Some use frame-time percentiles; others describe an average of the slowest one percent of frames converted into an FPS-like figure. That matters when comparing numbers from different sources.

For beginners: think of it as an attempt to answer “How bad does the slow part get?” But a single percentile-like number does not reveal where hitches occurred, how often they clustered, or what caused them. Raw per-frame timing is more informative when a detailed analysis is needed.

If a game stutters, what should you check?

  1. Look at the pattern. Is it one sudden spike, a recurring periodic hitch, or persistent unevenness?
  2. Observe the moment. Did the problem coincide with loading, turning the camera, opening a menu or a demanding scene?
  3. Write down conditions. Game version, graphics settings, resolution, driver, Proton runtime and overlay settings can change what happens.
  4. Check whether the instrumentation changes behavior. Measurement tools are part of the experiment and can impose overhead or bugs.
  5. Avoid jumping to a cause. A spike is not proof that the GPU, shader compiler, Proton or Linux itself is defective.

Linux Observatory's continuous footage is useful here: a viewer can watch what was happening and compare that moment with visible readouts rather than relying on a headline average alone.

Where to go deeper

MangoHud can show and log frame-time information. For its supported display options and logging behavior, see the upstream MangoHud documentation. DXVK has its own monitoring options and workload-specific behavior; see the DXVK project documentation.

Takeaway

FPS answers “how many?” Frame time helps answer “how evenly?” Neither one, by itself, answers “why?”
